<h1>Is NJ a no-fault state?</h1>

<section id="short-answer">
<p><strong>Yes, New Jersey is a no-fault state, but only for your medical bills.</strong> After a car accident, your own auto policy's Personal Injury Protection (PIP) pays your medical expenses no matter who caused it. No-fault does not mean nobody is at fault, and it does not mean you cannot sue. A claim against the driver who caused the car accident, for pain and suffering and for losses PIP does not pay, is still decided by fault and by the lawsuit option on your own policy.</p>
<p class="source">Source: NJ Department of Banking and Insurance (DOBI), <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">New Jersey Auto Insurance Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, p. 2: PIP "is sometimes called 'no-fault' coverage because it pays your own medical expenses no matter who caused the auto accident." Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>New Jersey's Supreme Court says the same thing: PIP medical benefits "are paid without regard to who caused the accident, giving rise to the moniker 'no-fault' insurance."</p>
<p class="source">Source: <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1480699/zabilowicz-v-kelsey/">Zabilowicz v. Kelsey, 200 N.J. 507 (2009)</a>, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
</section>

<section id="no-fault-vs-not-at-fault">
<h2>"No-fault" and "not at fault" are different things</h2>
<p>People mix these up all the time. "No-fault" is a type of insurance: your own PIP pays your medical bills without anyone deciding fault first. "Not at fault" is a finding about who caused the car accident. That finding still matters in New Jersey, because it decides whether, and how much, the other driver's insurance company pays you for everything PIP does not cover.</p>
<p>So the useful question after a car accident is not only "who pays my doctor?" It is three separate questions, and each one usually has its own claim file and its own insurance company contact.</p>

<section id="what-pip-pays">
<h2>What your PIP pays, and its limits</h2>
<p>PIP has two parts: medical expense benefits, and additional benefits such as income continuation, essential services, a death benefit and a funeral expense benefit. On the standard policy, most drivers buy a $250,000 medical limit per person per accident. Cheaper options are $150,000, $75,000, $50,000 or $15,000.</p>
<p>Even with a lower limit, the standard policy still pays up to $250,000 for certain permanent or significant injuries, such as brain or spinal cord injuries. The standard deductible is $250. On top of the deductible there is a 20 percent co-payment on medical bills between the deductible and $5,000.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 2, 5 to 7 and 12; DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">Standard Policy page</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>If you elected your health insurance as primary, your health plan pays first and PIP pays second. Your plan's deductibles, co-pays and pre-approval rules then apply. Medicare and Medicaid cannot be chosen as primary. The Basic Policy carries only $15,000 of PIP (with up to $250,000 for certain serious injuries).</p>
<p class="source">Sources: DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/pipoption.htm">Selecting Your Health Insurer for PIP Option</a>; DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/basicpolicy.shtml">Basic Policy page</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>More detail: <a href="/new-jersey/pip-coverage-limits-medical-bills-new-jersey">New Jersey PIP limits, deductibles and co-pays</a> and <a href="/new-jersey/pip-health-insurance-medical-bills-new-jersey">whether PIP or your health insurance pays first</a>.</p>

<h3>Medical bills bigger than your PIP limit</h3>
<p>Since a 2019 change to the law, medical bills that go beyond your PIP limit, or that PIP does not cover, can be claimed from the at-fault driver. What you cannot do is collect the same bill twice. In May 2026 the NJ Supreme Court added a limit: future treatment that will fit inside your remaining PIP limit must go through PIP, not the claim against the other driver.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10854078/lakita-d-murray-v-christopher-b-punina/">Murray v. Punina, A-51-24 (N.J. May 6, 2026)</a>, quoting N.J.S.A. 39:6A-12 as amended by L. 2019, cc. 244 and 245, which overturned <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8269493/haines-v-taft/">Haines v. Taft, 237 N.J. 271 (2019)</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
</section>

<section id="can-you-sue">
<h2>Can you sue the other driver in a no-fault state?</h2>
<p>Yes, but your own policy sets the rules for pain and suffering. Every New Jersey driver with PIP picks one of two lawsuit options on the Coverage Selection Form.</p>

<tr><th scope="row">Limitation on lawsuit (often called the "verbal threshold" or "limited right to sue")</th><td>Only for death, dismemberment, significant disfigurement or scarring, displaced fractures, loss of a fetus, or a permanent injury</td><td>Cheaper. It is the default if you never chose in writing. Basic Policy and Special Automobile Insurance Policy holders have it.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">No limitation on lawsuit (the "unlimited right to sue")</th><td>For any injury caused by someone else's negligence</td><td>Costs more. It does not guarantee any payment.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
</div>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8, text via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (archived 2026-05-01)</a>; N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8.1 (default election), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20240515013024/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8-1/">Justia 2023 edition (archived)</a>; DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 10 to 11. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Under the statute, an injury is permanent when the body part or organ "has not healed to function normally and will not heal to function normally with further medical treatment." If you sue under the limitation option, your side must serve a doctor's certification based on objective clinical evidence. Important: the limitation option restricts only pain and suffering claims. It does not stop a claim for economic losses, such as unpaid medical bills or lost wages.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (archived)</a>, consistent with the quotation in <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1480699/zabilowicz-v-kelsey/">Zabilowicz v. Kelsey</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Your lawsuit option also covers resident family members who are not named on another auto policy. To check yours, look at the Coverage Selection Form you signed or ask your own insurance company. Do not rely on the other driver's adjuster to tell you. How courts have applied the threshold in real appeals is on our <a href="/new-jersey/nj-verbal-threshold-appeals-rulings">verbal threshold appeals page</a>, and what counts as proof is on <a href="/new-jersey/nj-10-ways-to-prove-pain-and-suffering-after-an-accident">how to prove pain and suffering in New Jersey</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="fault-still-matters">
<h2>Fault still matters: the comparative fault rule</h2>
<p>Being partly at fault does not end your claim in New Jersey. Your own negligence does not bar recovery if it was not greater than the negligence of the driver you claim against. Your damages are then reduced by your share of fault. If your share is greater (51 percent or more against one driver), you recover nothing from that driver.</p>
<p>DOBI gives a simple example with $1,000 of damage: 10 percent at fault, you get $900; 50 percent at fault, you get $500; 80 percent at fault, you get nothing from the other driver. Insurance companies set fault from all the facts, not from the police report alone.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1, via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20261009074503/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-15-5-1/">Justia 2025 edition (archived 2026-10-09)</a>; DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/everythingauto2026.pdf">Everything You Wanted to Know About Auto Insurance (2026)</a>, pp. 26 to 27; DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/ins_ombudsman/ombuds_acnsfaq.html">Comparative Negligence Settlements FAQ</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Real fault splits from New Jersey appeals are on <a href="/new-jersey/partly-at-fault-nj-car-accident-real-fault-splits">our partly at fault page</a>. How to get the police report is on <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-report">how to get your New Jersey car accident report</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="special-cases">
<h2>Out-of-state and uninsured drivers</h2>
<p><strong>The other driver has no insurance or too little.</strong> Your uninsured or underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage pays you, not them. UIM pays up to the difference between your UIM limit and the other driver's liability limit. The Basic Policy has no UM/UIM. See <a href="/new-jersey/nj-uninsured-motorist-claim-help">uninsured motorist claims in New Jersey</a>.</p>
<p class="source">Source: DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 3, 5 and 8. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p><strong>You or the other driver is from out of state.</strong> If an out-of-state driver's insurance company is authorized in New Jersey, the policy must give NJ minimum coverage, including PIP, while the car is used here. That driver is usually held to the limitation on lawsuit option, even with full tort at home. A driver whose insurance company is not authorized in New Jersey is outside this rule, and cannot use the threshold as a defense.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 17:28-1.4 as quoted in <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7404094/cooper-hospital-university-v-prudential-insurance/">Cooper Hospital v. Prudential, 378 N.J. Super. 510 (App. Div. 2005)</a>; <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2258880/whitaker-v-devilla/">Whitaker v. DeVilla, 147 N.J. 341 (1997)</a>; <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1480699/zabilowicz-v-kelsey/">Zabilowicz v. Kelsey (2009)</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
</section>

<section id="next-steps">
<h2>What to do next</h2>
<ol>
<li>Get medical attention first. Do not wait for the other driver's insurance company to accept fault. Call 911 in an emergency.</li>
<li>Open a PIP claim with your own insurance company (or your health plan, if you made it primary). Keep the claim number.</li>
<li>Find your lawsuit option and your PIP limit on your Coverage Selection Form, or ask your insurance company.</li>
<li>Keep the treatment file, the car damage file and any injury claim against the other driver separate. Write down who you spoke to, the reference number and what they said in writing. Our <a href="/new-jersey/medical-records-injury-claim-new-jersey">medical records guide</a> lists what to keep.</li>
<li>Watch the clock. Injury suits generally must start within two years (N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2), but some deadlines are much shorter, for example 90 days to notify a public entity. Your deadline depends on your facts.</li>
</ol>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2, via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260930102816/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-14-2/">Justia 2025 edition (archived 2026-09-30)</a>; N.J.S.A. 59:8-8 as quoted in <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8267675/odonnell-v-nj-tpk-auth/">O'Donnell v. N.J. Turnpike Auth., 236 N.J. 335 (2019)</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<section id="faq">
<h2>Questions people ask</h2>
<h3>Is personal injury protection the same as bodily injury coverage?</h3>
<p>No. PIP pays your own medical bills regardless of fault. Bodily injury liability pays other people you hurt in a car accident you cause, including their pain and suffering and lost wages (DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, p. 2).</p>
<h3>Can you still sue if you have limited tort?</h3>
<p>Yes. With the limitation on lawsuit option you can claim pain and suffering only if your injury fits one of the six categories in N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a). Claims for economic losses, such as unpaid medical bills and lost wages, are not limited by that option.</p>
<h3>What does "no limitation on lawsuit" mean?</h3>
<p>It means you kept the right to claim pain and suffering for any injury someone else negligently caused (N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(b)). It costs more and does not guarantee any payment.</p>
<h3>Does no-fault mean I cannot be found at fault?</h3>
<p>No. Fault is decided for claims against the other driver, not for PIP or your own collision coverage. Under N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 your recovery is reduced by your share of fault, and you get nothing if your share is greater than the share of the driver, or drivers combined, you claim against.</p>
<h3>What is the statute of limitations for personal injury claims in New Jersey?</h3>
<p>Generally two years under N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2. Claims against a public entity need a notice within 90 days (N.J.S.A. 59:8-8), and suits over PIP benefits have their own clock. Exceptions exist, so do not count on two years without checking your facts.</p>
