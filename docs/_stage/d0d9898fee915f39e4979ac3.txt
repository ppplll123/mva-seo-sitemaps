<h1>NJ drunk driving accident help: your options if you were hurt</h1>

<section id="short-answer">
<p><strong>If a drunk driver hit you in New Jersey, your own PIP coverage pays your medical bills first, whoever caused the car accident.</strong> Separately, you may have an injury claim against the drunk driver's bodily injury liability insurance, which is decided on fault and your lawsuit option, not on the criminal case. Get medical attention, get the police report and keep your own records while the DWI case runs on its own track.</p>
<p class="source">Source: NJ Department of Banking and Insurance (DOBI), <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">New Jersey Auto Insurance Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, p. 2 (PIP "pays your own medical expenses no matter who caused the auto accident"; bodily injury liability "compensates others for pain, suffering and economic damages"). Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>This is general information, not legal advice.</p>
</section>

<section id="quiz-mid" class="quiz-cta">
<p>Hurt by a driver who had been drinking? Your PIP pays your medical bills either way. The questionnaire is about whether the other driver's insurance may also owe you. It asks about your injury, any lawyer you already have, fault, medical attention, the police report and the date. If your answers fit, a sponsoring lawyer or legal consultant may call you. It is free and there is no obligation.</p>

<section id="lawsuit-option">
<h2>Can you sue a drunk driver for pain and suffering?</h2>
<p>That depends on the lawsuit option on your own auto policy, not on the other driver's drinking. With the "no limitation on lawsuit" option, you can claim pain and suffering for any injury. With the "limitation on lawsuit" option, pain and suffering is claimable only for listed injuries: death, dismemberment, significant disfigurement or scarring, displaced fractures, loss of a fetus, or a permanent injury.</p>
<p>The limitation does not stop claims for economic loss, such as medical bills PIP did not pay or lost wages. If you never chose in writing, the law treats you as having the limitation option. Basic Policy holders also have it.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8 (text via Justia 2025 edition, <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Wayback snapshot 2026-05-01</a>); N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8.1(b) (<a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20240515013024/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8-1/">Wayback snapshot 2024-05-15</a>); DOBI <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 10 to 11, "The Right to Sue". Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Check which option you have on your Coverage Selection Form or declarations page. Our guide to <a href="/new-jersey/is-nj-a-no-fault-state">New Jersey no-fault and the lawsuit option</a> explains both choices.</p>
</section>

<section id="if-partly-at-fault">
<h2>What if the insurance company says you were partly at fault?</h2>
<p>You can still recover if your share of fault is not greater than the other driver's. Your damages are reduced by your percentage. DOBI's example: 10 percent at fault on 1,000 dollars of damage means 900 dollars paid. Insurance companies must have an internal appeal for fault-percentage disputes, with escalation to DOBI's Office of Insurance Claims Ombudsman.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 (text via Justia 2025 edition, <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20261009074503/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-15-5-1/">Wayback snapshot 2026-10-09</a>); DOBI, <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/ins_ombudsman/ombuds_acnsfaq.html">Comparative Negligence Settlements FAQ</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>See <a href="/new-jersey/partly-at-fault-nj-car-accident-real-fault-splits">how partial fault splits work in New Jersey</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="drunk-driver-own-claim">
<h2>Can the drunk driver claim against you?</h2>
<p>New Jersey law limits that. In Castano v. Augustine (2023), the Appellate Division read N.J.S.A. 39:6A-4.5(b) as barring a driver's claims for economic and noneconomic loss only after a conviction or guilty plea for drunk driving (or breath-test refusal). Evidence of a high blood alcohol level, without a conviction or plea, was not enough.</p>
<p class="source">Source: Castano v. Augustine, No. A-3925-21 (N.J. App. Div. Mar. 6, 2023), <a href="https://njlaw.rutgers.edu/courts/appellate/a3925-21.opn.pdf">slip opinion (Rutgers Law Library)</a>, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
</section>

<section id="njdot-counts">
<h2>How many NJ crash reports flag alcohol?</h2>
<p>Police crash reports in New Jersey include an alcohol-involved field. In 2023 and 2024 combined, 12,472 crashes carried that flag, about 2.4 percent of all reported crashes. They made up about 12 percent of fatal crashes in both years.</p>

<section id="next-steps">
<h2>What to do now</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Get medical attention</strong> and tell the clinician how the car accident happened. Call 911 in an emergency. Your PIP claim needs those records; see <a href="/new-jersey/medical-records-injury-claim-new-jersey">medical records for an NJ injury claim</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Open your PIP claim</strong> with your own insurance company. Check your PIP limit, since many drivers choose less than 250,000 dollars; see <a href="/new-jersey/pip-coverage-limits-medical-bills-new-jersey">PIP limits and medical bills</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Get the police report.</strong> State Police, Turnpike and Parkway reports are on the NJSP portal; local police reports come from the department that responded. See <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-report">how to get your NJ car accident report</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Keep your own record</strong> of what you saw, separate from what others told you. Note witnesses, photos and where cameras were. See <a href="/new-jersey/nj-what-evidence-helps-prove-fault-after-an-nj-crash">evidence that helps prove fault</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Find out the drunk driver's insurance.</strong> If there was none, or too little, look at your own <a href="/new-jersey/nj-uninsured-motorist-claim-help">uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the deadlines.</strong> An injury suit generally must be filed within two years. If a public entity's vehicle was involved, a notice of claim is due within 90 days. Exceptions exist, so do not wait for the DWI case to end.</li>
</ol>
