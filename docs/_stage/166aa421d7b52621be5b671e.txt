<section id="by-hour">
<h2>What time of day do deer crashes happen?</h2>
<p>Mostly after dark: 59 percent of animal crashes in 2023 and 2024 happened in darkness, and the 5 pm to 9 pm stretch alone held 32 percent.</p>
<ul>
<li>6 pm to 7 pm was the worst single hour, with 1,773 animal crashes in two years.</li>
<li>A smaller morning peak runs from 5 am to 8 am, with 3,466 crashes.</li>
<li>Midday is quiet: each hour from 9 am to 3 pm saw about 300 to 450.</li>
</ul>
<p>In October to December, 6 pm to 7 pm alone took 10 percent of the season's animal crashes. For all crash types by hour, see <a href="/new-jersey/when-do-most-car-accidents-happen">when most car accidents happen in New Jersey</a>.</p>
</section>
<section id="injuries">
<h2>How often is someone hurt when they hit a deer?</h2>
<p>Rarely, compared with other crashes: 914 of the 20,082 animal crashes in 2023 and 2024 hurt or killed someone, 4.6 percent, against 22 percent of all New Jersey crashes.</p>
<p>Those 914 crashes injured 1,012 people. Three animal crashes were fatal, all in 2024, killing three people. Across all 514,320 crash records of those two years, police coded a deer as the first thing struck on 18,591.</p>
</section>

<section id="report">
<h2>Do you have to report hitting a deer in NJ?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Yes, if anyone is hurt or killed, or property damage to any one person tops $500.</strong> Tell the local, county or State Police by the quickest means available.</p>
<p>Not knowing how bad the damage was is no defense if you knew you crashed. Knowingly failing to report can bring a $30 to $100 fine and a possible license or registration suspension. The 10-day written report and how to get a copy are on <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-report">how to get your NJ accident report</a>.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup></p>

</section>
<section id="no-police">
<h2>How do you report hitting a deer without a police report?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Use NJDOT's SR-1 self-reporting form.</strong> It is the only form for a crash police did not investigate, and it can be filed online.</p>
<p>NJDOT says the instant acknowledgment can be submitted to an insurance company, so keep it with your claim papers. Only police complete the NJTR-1 crash report.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup></p>

</section>
<section id="who-pays">
<h2>Who pays after you hit a deer in NJ?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">For injuries, your own Personal Injury Protection (PIP) pays your medical expenses no matter who caused the crash.</strong> A deer leaves no other driver to claim against.</p>
<p>PIP covers medical expenses and benefits such as income continuation. Repairs depend on your physical damage coverage. The Basic Policy offers collision and comprehensive only as options from some insurers, and the SAIP "dollar-a-day" policy has neither.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup></p>

<p>How a claim for the car works is on our <a href="/new-jersey/nj-new-jersey-property-damage-claim-help-and-steps-guide">New Jersey property damage claim steps</a>. Why PIP pays first is on <a href="/new-jersey/is-nj-a-no-fault-state">is NJ a no-fault state</a>.</p>
</section>
<section id="swerve">
<h2>Who is at fault if you swerve to miss a deer and hit another car?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Our sources give no deer rule.</strong> Fault is shared out by comparative negligence: you can recover from the other driver only if your share is not greater than theirs.</p>
<p>Your damages are then cut by your share. DOBI's examples: 10 percent at fault with $1,000 of damage, you get $900; 50 percent, $500; 80 percent, nothing from the other driver.</p>
<p>Insurers set the split from all the facts, not the police report alone, and must offer an internal appeal of a fault percentage. If you hit a pole or tree instead, there is no other driver, so PIP and your own physical damage coverage are the routes.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup></p>

<p>Real fault splits are on <a href="/new-jersey/partly-at-fault-nj-car-accident-real-fault-splits">partly at fault in a NJ car accident</a>.</p>
</section>
<section id="steps">
<h2>What should you do if you hit a deer in NJ?</h2>
<p>Call the police when the law requires it, then make sure a record of the crash exists, because your insurance company will work from it.</p>
<ol class="df-steps">
<li>Call the police if anyone is hurt or the damage is over $500.</li>
<li>Note which agency responded. State Police reports cover the Parkway, the Turnpike and the non-toll roads State Police patrol.</li>
<li>If no officer came, file the SR-1 form and keep the acknowledgment.</li>
<li>Get the police report and check it. DOBI says to tell your insurer and the police department about any mistakes.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup></li>
</ol>

</section>
<section id="faq" class="df-faqs">
<h2>Questions people ask</h2>
<details class="df-faq" open><summary><h3>What is a non-reportable accident in NJ?</h3></summary>
<p>One where no one is hurt or killed and no one person has more than $500 of property damage. A deer strike like that needs no police call, and if no officer investigated, the SR-1 is the form that records it.</p>
</details><details class="df-faq"><summary><h3>Is hitting a deer an accident?</h3></summary>
<p>When police investigate one, NJDOT records code it as crash type "Animal": 20,082 in 2023 and 2024. Whether your insurer handles it under comprehensive or collision is not answered here, because our sources do not say.</p>
</details><details class="df-faq"><summary><h3>Are there deer in New Jersey?</h3></summary>
<p>Yes. Police coded a deer as the first thing struck in all 21 counties in 2023 and 2024, from 23 records in Hudson to 2,252 in Monmouth.</p>
