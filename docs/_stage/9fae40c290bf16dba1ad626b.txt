<h2>How did New Jersey juries actually split fault?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Injured people at 20 and 40 percent fault kept reduced awards.</strong> Those at 51 and 75 percent got nothing. The ladder below runs from the smallest share on the injured person to the largest.</p>
<p>The Dutton estate at 40 percent and the Brooks estate at 51 percent sit 11 points apart, and that gap decided $364,800 versus nothing. Rice and Dutton both turned on where the injured person was and what the driver could see; the <a href="/new-jersey/hit-by-car-pedestrian-cyclist-nj-appeals">pedestrian and cyclist appeals</a> page covers them in full.</p>

<section id="dobi-examples">
<h2>How much do you get at 10, 50 or 80 percent fault?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">At 10 percent you collect $900 of $1,000 in damage, at 50 percent $500, and at 80 percent nothing from the other driver.</strong> These are DOBI's own worked examples.</p>
<div class="table-wrap">

</tbody>

</div>
<p>The rule behind these numbers is modified comparative negligence: you recover if your fault is not greater than the other side's, minus your own percentage. The same cut applies to other costs you claim, such as rental car charges.</p>
<p>Some pages say a partly at fault driver "can recover up to 50 percent" of their losses. That misreads the rule: the cut equals your share, so at 10 percent fault you keep 90 percent.</p>
<p>Who pays for your car damage? Under your own collision coverage, your fault share does not reduce the payment; against the other driver, it does. See the <a href="/new-jersey/nj-new-jersey-property-damage-claim-help-and-steps-guide">property damage claim steps</a>.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup></p>

</section>
<section id="jury">
<h2>Can you recover if you are exactly 50 percent at fault?</h2>
<p>Yes. At exactly 50 percent you still recover half, because your share is not greater than the other side's.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-1446-23.html">Koukounias hit-and-run UM trial (2025, unpublished)</a> shows the line: the jury split fault 50/50 and stopped. The judge sent it back, saying her share was exactly fifty percent and so not greater than fifty. The rest of that case is on the <a href="/new-jersey/nj-hit-and-run-accident-claim-help">hit-and-run claim help</a> page.</p>
</section>
<section id="two-drivers">
<h2>Who pays when two other drivers share the blame?</h2>
<p>A defendant 60 percent or more at fault can be made to pay all your damages; below 60 percent, each pays only its own share.</p>
<p>In Carbajal the identified driver at 60 percent paid the balance after the phantom driver's UM payment. For chain-reaction crashes see <a href="/new-jersey/describe-multiple-vehicle-crash-nj">help after a multiple-vehicle car accident</a>, and for fled or uninsured drivers <a href="/new-jersey/nj-uninsured-motorist-claim-help">how uninsured motorist claims work</a>.</p>
</section>
<section id="insurer-percentage">
<h2>Who decides your fault percentage, and can you appeal it?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Most claims never reach a jury: the other driver's insurance company sets the percentage.</strong> You can challenge it through the insurer's internal appeal, the DOBI Claims Ombudsman or court.</p>
<p>The steps and what to say are on <a href="/new-jersey/nj-what-to-expect-from-an-insurance-adjuster-after-an-accident">what to expect from an insurance adjuster</a>. Keep the written fault decision, and read <a href="/new-jersey/nj-8-questions-to-ask-before-accepting-a-settlement-offer">questions to ask before accepting a settlement offer</a> before you sign a release.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup></p>

</section>
<section id="fault-check">
<h2>Which facts moved the percentage in these cases?</h2>
<p>Three kinds of fact moved the splits above: where the injured person was, other vehicles, and alcohol evidence.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Position and visibility:</strong> Rice (75 percent) and Dutton (40 percent) turned on where the person was and what the driver could see.</li>
<li><strong>Other vehicles, including one that fled:</strong> other drivers' shares changed who paid in Carbajal, Cain and Robinson.</li>
<li><strong>Alcohol evidence:</strong> it became a comparative negligence defence in Brooks, where the driver ended at 51 percent.</li>
</ul>
<p>For police reports, statements and other proof, see <a href="/new-jersey/nj-what-evidence-helps-prove-fault-after-an-nj-crash">what evidence helps prove fault after a New Jersey crash</a>, and get your <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-report">New Jersey car accident report</a>.</p>
</section>
<section id="other-rules">
<h2>Does PIP pay regardless of fault?</h2>
<p>Yes. PIP pays eligible medical bills whoever caused the crash; fault only matters for what you claim from the other driver.</p>
<ul>
<li>PIP limits: see <a href="/new-jersey/pip-coverage-limits-medical-bills-new-jersey">PIP limits and medical bills in New Jersey</a>.</li>
<li>Lawsuit option: with the limitation on lawsuit option, pain and suffering also needs a threshold injury. See <a href="/new-jersey/nj-verbal-threshold-appeals-rulings">how the verbal threshold worked in 13 rulings</a>.</li>
<li>Local data: Rice and Brooks were tried in Camden County. See <a href="/new-jersey/camden-county-car-accident-help">Camden County car accident help</a>.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup></li>
</ul>

</section>
<section id="faq" class="df-faqs">
<h2>FAQ</h2>
<details class="df-faq" open><summary><h3>Is NJ an at-fault state for car accidents?</h3></summary>
<p>Both. New Jersey is no-fault for PIP medical bills, and comparative fault applies when you claim against the other driver. See <a href="/new-jersey/is-nj-a-no-fault-state">how New Jersey no-fault insurance works</a>.</p>
</details><details class="df-faq"><summary><h3>What happens if the other driver is at fault?</h3></summary>
<p>If the other driver carries more than half the fault, you can claim from their insurance company, reduced by any share put on you. Robinson and Cain show injured drivers at 0 percent keeping a $3.5 million verdict and a $325,000 judgment.</p>
</details><details class="df-faq"><summary><h3>Who is most at fault in a rear-end collision?</h3></summary>
<p>Not always the rear driver. In Cain, a three-car rear-end crash after an aborted U-turn and sudden stop, the jury put 65 percent on the U-turning driver and 35 percent on the following driver. See <a href="/new-jersey/rear-end-crash-new-jersey">rear-end accident help in New Jersey</a>.</p>
</details><details class="df-faq"><summary><h3>Should I get a lawyer for a car accident that wasn't my fault?</h3></summary>
<p>A lawyer's review can matter most when the insurer puts your share near the 50 percent line: in Brooks, 51 percent meant nothing at all.</p>
</details></section>
