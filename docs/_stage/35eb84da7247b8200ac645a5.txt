<h1>10 ways to prove pain and suffering after a New Jersey car accident</h1>

<section id="short-answer">
<p><strong>You prove pain and suffering with medical records that show what the car accident did to your body, plus a steady, honest record of how it changed your daily life.</strong> First, check which lawsuit option your auto policy has. If it is the "Limited Right to Sue" option, you can claim pain and suffering from the driver who caused the car accident only if your injury fits a short list, such as a displaced fracture, significant scarring or a permanent injury backed by objective medical proof.</p>
<p class="source">Source: NJ Department of Banking and Insurance (DOBI), <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">Standard Auto Insurance Policy, "The Right to Sue"</a>, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>New Jersey law calls pain and suffering "noneconomic loss." It is separate from your medical bills and lost wages, which are economic losses.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8, statute text via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback snapshot 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>This is general information, not legal advice.</p>
</section>

<section id="lawsuit-option">
<h2>Step one: find out what you have to prove</h2>
<p>Every New Jersey Standard Policy holder picks one of two lawsuit options on the Coverage Selection Form. The choice also covers your spouse, children and other relatives living with you who are not on another auto policy. If nobody made a written choice, the law treats the policy as Limited Right to Sue. Basic Policy and Special Automobile Insurance Policy (SAIP) holders always have the limitation on lawsuit option.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">DOBI Standard Policy page</a>; <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">DOBI New Jersey Auto Insurance Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 4-5, 10-11; N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8.1 via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20240515013024/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8-1/">Justia 2023 edition (Wayback snapshot 2024-05-15)</a>, secondary copy. All retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<h3>What "permanent" means under the limited option</h3>
<p>Under the statute, an injury counts as permanent when the body part or organ has not healed to work normally and will not do so with more medical treatment. DOBI's page adds that this must rest on "objective medical proof." Feeling pain every day is real, but the law asks a narrower question: will the injured part work normally again?</p>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy; <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">DOBI Standard Policy page</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<h3>The physician certification</h3>
<p>If a lawsuit is filed under the limited option, the injured person must serve a doctor's certification within 60 days after the other driver files an answer. The certification must be based on objective clinical evidence. A court can allow one more period of up to 60 days for good cause. A false certification is a fourth-degree crime.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<h3>What you do not have to prove</h3>
<p>You do not have to show a "serious life impact." That was a test under the older threshold. The New Jersey Supreme Court held in DiProspero v. Penn, 183 N.J. 477 (2005), that it does not apply to the current threshold adopted in 1998.</p>
<p class="source">Source: Zabilowicz v. Kelsey, 200 N.J. 507 (2009), note 4, citing DiProspero v. Penn, <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1480699/zabilowicz-v-kelsey/">CourtListener</a>, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Two more points can change the answer. If the driver who hit you is from out of state and not in the New Jersey PIP system, that driver may not be able to use the threshold against you. And if you were partly at fault, you can still recover, but your damages are reduced by your share of fault, and you get nothing from that driver if your share is greater than theirs.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1480699/zabilowicz-v-kelsey/">Zabilowicz v. Kelsey, 200 N.J. 507 (2009)</a>; N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20261009074503/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-15-5-1/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-10-09)</a>, secondary copy; <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/everythingauto2026.pdf">DOBI "Everything You Wanted to Know About Auto Insurance" (2026)</a>, pp. 26-27. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<section id="ten-ways">
<h2>The 10 ways to prove pain and suffering</h2>
<p>None of these guarantees a claim or a payment. They help make sure the record shows what really happened. Never get treatment you do not need just to build a file.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Get medical attention and describe every symptom.</strong> Tell the clinician what hurts, when it started and what it stops you doing. Symptoms left out of early records are harder to connect to the car accident later.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the objective findings, not only the pain scores.</strong> Under the limited option, the doctor's certification must rest on objective clinical evidence. Keep imaging reports, test results and exam findings such as measured range of motion. Your own reading of a scan is not evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Ask your treating doctor the permanency question directly.</strong> Ask whether the injured part has healed to work normally, and whether further treatment is expected to fix it. Ask the doctor to write the answer down. That is the statute's test, word for word.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the treatment plan and keep the dates.</strong> A record of visits, referrals and instructions shows how the injury developed. If you miss care because of transport, work or cost, tell your provider so the real reason is in the record.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose old injuries and conditions.</strong> If the car accident made an old problem worse, the records need to say so in medical terms. In one 2025 appeal, a jury instruction on aggravation was not given because there was no medical evidence that the car accident aggravated the earlier condition.</li>
<li><strong>Link the injury to this car accident.</strong> Keep the crash report, photos and your first medical notes together. In two 2025 appeals in the table below, the claim failed on causation: the injured person could not show the car accident caused the injury.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a short dated journal.</strong> Write what you could not do, what needed help and how you slept. Write it close to the day. Mark later memories as written later. Record good days and improvement too.</li>
<li><strong>Compare before and after.</strong> Note what you did before the car accident, such as work tasks, sports, childcare or chores, and what changed. Keep work changes separate from lost pay, which is an economic loss.</li>
<li><strong>Keep photos of visible injuries and scars over time.</strong> Significant scarring and significant disfigurement are listed injuries under the limited option. Keep the original files with their dates. Do not stage photos.</li>
<li><strong>Ask people who saw the change to write their own account.</strong> A spouse, coworker or friend can describe what they saw, in their own words. Do not write it for them.</li>
</ol>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy, retrieved 2026-11-10 (items 2, 3, 9). Appeals: see the table below (items 5, 6).</p>
<p>For a checklist of documents, see <a href="/new-jersey/medical-records-injury-claim-new-jersey">which medical records to keep for a car accident injury claim</a>. For photos, see <a href="/new-jersey/nj-how-to-use-photos-and-videos-after-a-car-accident">how to use photos and videos after a car accident</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="not-pain-and-suffering">
<h2>What pain and suffering does not cover</h2>
<p>Your own Personal Injury Protection (PIP) pays your medical bills no matter who caused the car accident, up to your PIP limit. That part does not depend on your lawsuit option.</p>
<p class="source">Source: <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">DOBI Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, p. 2; <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">DOBI Standard Policy page</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>The limited option does not stop a claim for economic loss, such as lost wages or medical bills your PIP does not cover. Since a 2019 change in the law, medical bills above or outside your PIP limits can be claimed from the driver at fault. In 2026 the New Jersey Supreme Court held that future medical costs that will stay within your remaining PIP limits must go through PIP instead.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy; <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10854078/lakita-d-murray-v-christopher-b-punina/">Murray v. Punina, A-51-24 (N.J. May 6, 2026)</a>. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Read more: <a href="/new-jersey/is-nj-a-no-fault-state">how New Jersey no-fault insurance works after a car accident</a>, <a href="/new-jersey/pip-coverage-limits-medical-bills-new-jersey">PIP limits and medical bills</a> and <a href="/new-jersey/nj-can-you-claim-lost-wages-after-a-car-accident-in-nj">claiming lost wages after a car accident</a>.</p>

<h3>Things that do not prove pain and suffering</h3>
<ul>
<li>A "multiplier" of your medical bills. The threshold statute and DOBI's guidance do not use one.</li>
<li>Someone else's settlement or verdict. Each case turns on its own medical proof, fault and policy.</li>
<li>The lawsuit option alone. Having the unlimited option does not guarantee compensation.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="faq">
<h2>Questions people ask</h2>
<h3>Can you still sue if you have limited tort?</h3>
<p>Yes. In New Jersey, limited tort is the "Limitation on Lawsuit" option. You can still sue for economic losses such as lost wages. You can sue for pain and suffering only if your injury is on the statute's list, such as a displaced fracture, significant scarring or a permanent injury certified by a doctor.</p>
<p class="source">Source: <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">DOBI Standard Policy page</a>, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<h3>What does "no limitation on lawsuit" mean?</h3>
<p>It means you keep the right to sue the driver who caused the car accident for pain and suffering for any injury. DOBI calls it the "Unlimited Right to Sue." It costs more than the limited option.</p>
<p class="source">Sources: <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/standardpolicy.html">DOBI Standard Policy page</a>; <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">DOBI Buyer's Guide 2026</a>, pp. 10-11. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<h3>Do soft-tissue injuries count as permanent?</h3>
<p>There is no rule that they never count or always count. The question is whether the body part will heal to work normally, shown by objective clinical evidence.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 39:6A-8(a), via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260501025515/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-39/section-39-6a-8/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-05-01)</a>, secondary copy, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<h3>What is the time limit for a car accident injury lawsuit in New Jersey?</h3>
<p>Generally two years, under N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2, with exceptions. Claims against public entities need notice within 90 days. Do not rely on a date you work out yourself.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 2A:14-2 via <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20260930102816/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-14-2/">Justia 2025 edition (Wayback 2026-09-30)</a>, secondary copy, retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
</section>
