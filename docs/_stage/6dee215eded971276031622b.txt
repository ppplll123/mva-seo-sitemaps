<section class="place-common df-rules" id="what-these-numbers-mean">
<h2>What these numbers can and cannot tell you</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">The counts on this page are police-reported car accidents from NJDOT crash records, analyzed by us.</strong> They show where and when accidents where someone was hurt happened in Jersey City. They do not show who was at fault in any accident, and they say nothing about whether you have a claim or what it could be worth.</p>
<details class="nj-rules"><summary>Rules that apply anywhere in New Jersey</summary>
<ul>
<li>Your own auto policy's PIP pays eligible medical expenses "no matter who caused the auto accident," up to your policy's limit, or pays second if you chose your health insurance as primary. A claim against the other driver for pain and suffering is separate and depends on fault and on the lawsuit option on your policy. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup></li>
<li>Being partly at fault does not stop you from recovering, as long as your share is not greater than the other driver's, or the combined share of all the drivers you claim against (the "51 percent" rule). Your damages are reduced by your share. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup></li>
<li>If someone is hurt or killed, or property damage to any one person is over $500, the driver must tell the police right away and, unless a police officer files a report, send the Motor Vehicle Commission a written report (the SR-1 form) within 10 days. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup></li>
<li>An injury lawsuit generally must be filed within two years, and a claim against a public body (a town, the state, NJ Transit) needs a written notice within 90 days. These are general rules with exceptions; check your own dates with a lawyer. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup></li>
</ul>
</details>
