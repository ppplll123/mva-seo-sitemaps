<section id="first-days">
<h2>I was hurt on I-295. What should I do in the first few days?</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">Most I-295 injury crashes are rear-end hits or a car going into a guide rail or tree.</strong> Both often come down to who caused it.</p>
<ol class="df-steps">
<li>Write down who saw it. Police came in 89% of the crashes we heard about, but only 28% of people had a witness's name and number. If you were rear-ended (hit from behind) at highway speed, a witness can say who braked and who did not.</li>
<li>Tell the officer if another car pushed you off the road, even if the cars never touched. Asked who was at fault, 44% said the other driver, 25% said themselves, and 32% were not sure.</li>
<li>Keep the report number, car photos and doctor visits in one place. People whose crash was under 3 months old when they reached out signed with a lawyer 18% of the time; at 1 to 2 years, 9.5%. Half waited 156 days or more.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup></li>
</ol>

<section id="over-the-bridge">
<h2>The crash was over the bridge in Delaware or Pennsylvania. Can a NJ lawyer still help?</h2>
<p>Usually not. I-295 starts at the Delaware Memorial Bridge in Pennsville and runs near the bridges into Philadelphia, so you can cross a state line without noticing. If you crashed on the other side, that state's rules and deadlines usually apply, so look for a lawyer licensed there.</p>
<p>In the signed car cases a law firm sent back with a known crash place, 25 of 39 happened outside New Jersey: 10 in Pennsylvania and 9 in New York. Our guide to a <a href="/new-jersey/car-accident-in-another-state-nj-resident">crash across the bridge in Pennsylvania or Delaware</a> explains what changes.<sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup></p>

<section id="state-police-reports">

<section id="by-year">

<section class="place-common df-rules" id="what-these-numbers-mean">
<h2>What these numbers can and cannot tell you</h2>
<p><strong class="df-k">The counts on this page are police-reported crashes from NJDOT records, counted by us.</strong> They show where and when people were hurt on I-295. They do not show who was at fault in any crash, and they cannot tell you whether you have a case or what it could be worth.</p>
<h3>Rules that apply anywhere in New Jersey</h3>
<ul>
<li>Your own car insurance pays your doctor bills first, whoever caused the crash. This part of your policy is called PIP, and you will see the word on your bills. It pays up to the limit you picked, after your deductible and co-pay. If you picked your health insurance to pay first, it does, and PIP pays second. Being paid for your pain by the other driver is separate: it depends on who was at fault and on the choice about suing that you made when you bought your policy. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup></li>
<li>If they say it was partly your fault, you can still be paid, as long as you were not more at fault than the other driver (people call this the "51 percent" rule). What you get is cut by your share of the blame. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup></li>
<li>If anyone is hurt or killed, or one person's car or property has more than $500 of damage, the driver must call the police right away and send a written report to the Motor Vehicle Commission within 10 days, unless an officer writes one. If no officer came, the state's SR-1 form is the one to send. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup></li>
<li>You usually have two years to sue for an injury. If a town, the state or NJ Transit was involved, you must send them a written notice within 90 days. There are exceptions, so check your own dates with a lawyer. <sup class="df-fn"><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup></li>
</ul>
