<h1>Partly at Fault in a NJ Car Accident? 40% Kept $364,800. 75% Got Zero.</h1>

<section id="short-answer">
<p><strong>Yes, you can still recover money in New Jersey if you were partly at fault for a car accident, as long as your share of fault is not greater than the other side's.</strong> Your recovery is cut by your own percentage. If you are found more than 50 percent at fault, you get nothing from the other driver.</p>
<p class="source">Source: N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1, <a href="http://web.archive.org/web/20261009074503/https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/title-2a/section-2a-15-5-1/">statute text (Justia 2025 edition, archived copy)</a>; NJ Department of Banking and Insurance (DOBI), <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/ins_ombudsman/ombuds_acnsfaq.html">Auto Comparative Negligence Settlement FAQ</a>, question 5. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>Your own PIP still pays your medical bills whoever caused the car accident. The fault split matters for what you can claim from the other driver's insurance company. This page shows how the rule works, with DOBI's own examples and eight real New Jersey court decisions.</p>
<p class="source">Source: DOBI, <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/pdf/autoguide2026.pdf">New Jersey Auto Insurance Buyer's Guide 2026 (PDF)</a>, page 2: PIP pays "your own medical expenses no matter who caused the auto accident." Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>

<section id="the-rule">
<h2>The New Jersey rule in three parts</h2>
<p>This is general information, not legal advice.</p>
<p>New Jersey's Comparative Negligence Act sets three rules that decide what a fault split does to the money.</p>
<div class="table-wrap">
<div class="l2-tablewrap" tabindex="0" role="region" aria-label="Scrollable table"><table>
<caption>What N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 to 5.3 say about fault splits</caption>
<thead>
<tr><th scope="col">Section</th><th scope="col">What it says</th><th scope="col">What it means for you</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><th scope="row">2A:15-5.1</th><td>Your own fault does not stop you from recovering as long as it is not greater than the fault of the person you claim against, or of all the people you claim against combined. Damages are reduced by your percentage.</td><td>Up to 50 percent at fault, you recover a reduced amount. More than 50 percent, you recover nothing from the other side.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">2A:15-5.2</th><td>The jury or judge sets your full damages and each party's percentage of fault. The percentages total 100.</td><td>Damages and fault are two separate findings. The reduction is applied after both are set.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">2A:15-5.3</th><td>A defendant 60 percent or more responsible can be made to pay the full damages. A defendant less than 60 percent responsible pays only its own percentage. Defendants have contribution rights among themselves.</td><td>When two or more drivers share the blame, who pays what depends on their shares, not only on yours.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>

Statute text read from these secondary copies; retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>So the line sits just above 50 percent. In Brooks v. Longcor (2025, unpublished) the jury put the driver who died at 51 percent and the truck defendants at 49 percent, and that meant a no-cause verdict. At exactly 50 percent you can still recover half (DOBI FAQ, question 5).</p>
<p class="source">Sources: N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.1 text above; <a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-3405-22.html">Brooks v. Patel (2021, published)</a></th><td>Injured driver 0. Identified driver 60, phantom vehicle 40.</td><td>$200,000 verdict. The trial judge ordered $120,000 from the driver plus the $15,000 UM limit. The appeals court reversed: the UM carrier pays $15,000 and the driver pays the $185,000 balance.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row"><a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6486568/morales-hurtado-v-reinoso/">Morales-Hurtado v. Reinoso (2018, published)</a></th><td>Injured driver 20, other driver 80 (rear-end crash of a jitney).</td><td>$50,000 for pain and suffering plus $71,615.73 in past medical costs, subject to the 20 percent reduction. The verdict was later reversed for a new trial over defence counsel's conduct.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row"><a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6487385/dutton-v-rando/">Dutton v. Rando (2019, published)</a></th><td>Cyclist 40, SUV driver 60.</td><td>$500,000 wrongful death plus $108,000 survivorship damages, molded to a $364,800 judgment after the 40 percent reduction ($608,000 x 60 percent).</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row"><a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-3405-22.html">Brooks v. Longcor (2025, unpublished)</a></th><td>Driver who died 51, truck owner and truck driver 49 (car hit a truck parked on the shoulder; the driver had a.054 blood alcohol level).</td><td>Nothing. The split "resulted in a no-cause verdict." The new trial motion was denied and the appeals court affirmed.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row"><a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6485067/rice-v-miller/">Rice v. Miller (2018, published)</a></th><td>Pedestrian 75, driver 25 (crossing a highway).</td><td>Nothing. Judgment for the driver because the pedestrian's share was greater.</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row"><a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-2565-23.html">Daniels v. Bussey (2025, unpublished)</a></th><td>Driver found not negligent (pedestrian crossing a road).</td><td>No recovery. The jury instructions on crosswalk status were upheld.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
</div>

Percentages and amounts checked on 2026-11-10 against the opinion texts and our coded table of decisions (reviewed 2026-11-05), part of our <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-appeals-index">76-decision New Jersey car accident appeals index</a>.</p>
<p>At 40 percent, the Dutton estate kept a $364,800 judgment. At 51 percent, the Brooks estate got nothing. These are different cases with different damages, but the line between them is the 50 percent rule. Rice and Dutton both turned on where the injured person was and what the driver could see.</p>

<section id="two-drivers">
<h2>When the blame is split between other drivers, not you</h2>
<p>In three of the eight decisions the injured driver had no fault and two or more others shared it. A defendant at 60 percent or more can be made to pay the full damages, then seek contribution from the others (N.J.S.A. 2A:15-5.3).</p>
<p>Carbajal is the edge case. The phantom driver could not be found, so the identified driver could not get contribution from anyone. The court still held she pays the balance after the $15,000 UM payment. A split like this decides legal responsibility. It does not guarantee collection, because policy limits, settlements and ability to pay still matter.</p>
<p>If a driver fled or had no insurance, read <a href="/new-jersey/nj-uninsured-motorist-claim-help">how uninsured motorist claims work in New Jersey</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="insurer-percentage">
<h2>An insurance company's percentage is not a court finding</h2>
<p>Most claims never reach a jury. DOBI says the other driver's insurance company investigates and assigns a percentage of fault. It may look at the police report, driver and witness statements, motor vehicle laws and the layout of the road. The police report does not decide fault on its own.</p>
<p>If you disagree with the percentage, DOBI sets out this route:</p>
<ol>
<li>Raise it first with the claims adjuster or the adjuster's supervisor.</li>
<li>File an internal appeal. Every New Jersey auto insurer must have an internal appeal process and must tell you how to use it.</li>
<li>If you are still unhappy, ask DOBI's Office of the Insurance Claims Ombudsman to review the claim (1-800-446-7467).</li>
<li>Disputes about the facts of the car accident are for the courts. You keep the right to file suit even after an appeal or an ombudsman review.</li>
</ol>
<p class="source">Source: DOBI, <a href="https://www.nj.gov/dobi/ins_ombudsman/ombuds_acnsfaq.html">Auto Comparative Negligence Settlement FAQ</a>, questions 3, 4, 6 and 7. Retrieved 2026-11-10.</p>
<p>A fault percentage does not measure your injury or prove lost wages. Keep the insurance company's written explanation and read the full release before you accept any offer. See <a href="/new-jersey/nj-8-questions-to-ask-before-accepting-a-settlement-offer">questions to ask before accepting a settlement offer</a>.</p>
</section>

<section id="jury">
<h2>What happened when juries split fault 50/50 and 51/49</h2>
<p>In <a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-1446-23.html">Koukounias v. NJM (2025, unpublished)</a>, a hit-and-run UM trial, the jury put the injured driver and the other driver at 50 percent each, then stopped. The verdict sheet said to stop only if the injured driver's share was greater than 50 percent. The judge told the jury her share "is not greater than [fifty] percent. In fact, it is exactly [fifty] percent," so it had to go on to the injury question. The jury then found no permanent injury caused by the accident.</p>
<p>On appeal the injured driver argued the judge should have given an "ultimate outcome charge," which tells the jury what its percentages do to the money. The opinion notes the Supreme Court said in Roman v. Mitchell that a comparative negligence jury "should be given an ultimate outcome charge." It held that leaving the charge out was not plain error here, because the verdict sheet and the judge's supplemental charge explained the jury's task. The judgment was affirmed.</p>
<p>In <a href="https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-unpublished/2025/a-3405-22.html">Brooks v. Longcor (2025, unpublished)</a>, a wrongful death case after a car hit a parked tractor-trailer, the defence raised comparative negligence based on the driver's blood alcohol. The jury found the driver 51 percent and the defendants 49 percent liable, a no-cause verdict. The appeals court rejected the estate's challenges to the evidence rulings and the denial of a new trial.</p>
</section>

<section id="method">
<h2>Method and data</h2>
<p>The eight decisions come from our 76-decision corpus of New Jersey car accident and coverage appeals: 52 published decisions from CourtListener (2018 to 2026, including 4 Law Division rulings) and 24 unpublished 2025 Appellate Division decisions from Justia. Seven state numerical fault splits and one is a no-negligence verdict. Brooks's 51/49 split was read from the opinion text on 2026-11-10; our coded dataset does not record it. Koukounias is discussed for a related point. Percentages and amounts are quoted from the opinions. <a href="https://sitemap.injuriescases.com/data/nj-research/nj-comparative-fault-appeals.csv">Download the nine-row dataset (spreadsheet file)</a> or browse the <a href="/new-jersey/nj-car-accident-appeals-index">full appeals index</a>.</p>
